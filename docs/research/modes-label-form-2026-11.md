## 0. Premise check — read this before anything else

The brief's §1 rests on five single draws and states of the `explain`/`explanation` contrast: "**1 of 5**, with **3 abstentions**" against "**4 of 5**". **That contrast is withdrawn by the commissioning programme's own record.** [`modes-experiment-log.md`](modes-experiment-log.md:30) lists it as a withdrawn claim ("Two single draws. Their Fisher p was 0.206"), and [`modes-experiment-log.md`](modes-experiment-log.md:396) is a correction entry posted _after_ the brief was written.

The relevant cells were then run properly at n = 100 per cell ([`modes-experiment-log.md`](modes-experiment-log.md:370)). On the comparison the brief was written to explain — `explain` + imperative-with-negation against `explanation` + declarative — the larger round produced **.440 against .300**, i.e. the noun cell picked the intended entry _less_ often and asked for information _more_ often (ask-only 50/100 against 44/100).

This report therefore does two things at once:

1. It answers the brief's four questions against the external literature, as asked.
2. It grades the lead against both the literature **and** the programme's corrected internal data, because a report that took the brief's premise at face value would be researching a direction the commissioning programme has already reversed.

Nothing else in the brief is altered; this file is the only artefact produced.

## 1. Executive answer

**No prior art exists for the lead as stated.** I found no study — human or machine — that compares a verb-named entry with a noun-named entry in a list of selectable options and measures the choice. Interface conventions that say "start with a verb" (Microsoft, GOV.UK, NN/g) are style guidance with no measurement behind them, and the one mature theory of label choice, information scent, predicts that _specificity_ matters and part of speech does not.

The nearest controlled evidence points against a form effect. In the only factor-isolated study of tool selection, **tool names rarely matter — 6 of 29 estimates exclude zero — and when names are the only difference, position decides the choice** (first-listed chosen in 78.7–96.0% of trials). Descriptions and parameter schemas move selection; names mostly do not.

**Abstention is the opposite case: heavily documented, and systematically mismeasured.** Asking rather than acting is a named expectation in the Berkeley function-calling benchmark, and its official scorer never checks the turn where the model is supposed to ask. That is the same measurement artefact the commissioning programme hit twice.

The conditional-ambiguity refinement is **directionally supported by two independent findings** — a vendor-documented case where naming the requested artefact ("suggest some changes") produces the artefact rather than the act, and a controlled study where description quality matters more for underspecified queries than for specific ones — but **neither tests label form**, and neither supports the specific polarity the brief's five draws suggested.

## 3. Graded findings per literature

### 3.1 HCI menu, command and button labelling

**The guidance is unanimous and unevidenced.** Microsoft's menu guidance says to "use verbs or noun phrases to label menu commands that represent actions", and adds an alternation rule — if the _menu_ is a verb, the _commands_ should be nouns, and vice versa — plus "always start commit button labels with a verb" for commit buttons **[verified — documented requirement]**; no measurement is cited anywhere in the guidance. GOV.UK's button component says to "write button text in sentence case, describing the action it performs", and lists canonical labels (`Save and continue`, `Accept and send`) **[verified — documented]**; again, no study. NN/g's command-naming guidance says to "lead with verbs or verb phrases that clearly outline what will happen" and to avoid generic labels **[reported — practitioner guidance]**.

So: **the verb-first rule is a convention with three-plus independent institutional endorsements and zero published measurements.** It is the strongest thing that can be said for it.

**The one theory that does predict label effects predicts specificity, not grammar.** Information foraging holds that a label is evaluated for _scent_ — how well it predicts the content behind it — and that users pick the highest-scent option and abandon a patch when scent is low. The best empirical demonstration I could reach measured that the semantic match between a sought item's name and the menu label under which it sat "was a key determinant of the decision to use the menus" against site search **[verified — measured; Katz & Byrne, ACM TOCHI 2003]**. Under this account, `explain` and `explanation` are near-equivalent: both name the same target domain, and neither is more specific about the _outcome_. The account predicts a difference only if one label carries information the other lacks — which, for this pair, it does not.

**NN/g states the mechanism most usefully, and against the noun/verb framing.** Forced parallel language is a common mistake, and "it's not the use of the verbs per se that is problematic. Rather, the vague, action-oriented verbs that are the issue": `Explore`, `Discover`, `Partner` offer "too little differentiation". Correspondingly, switching part of speech between informational (noun) and task-based (verb) categories can _help_ by signalling a section change **[reported]**. That is a claim about discriminability, and it is compatible with a null form effect.

**Two label properties do have measured support, and neither is grammatical form.** (i) _Informativeness/specificity_ — the scent result above, plus the menu-organization literature where semantic grouping beats alphabetic when the user is searching for a _function_ rather than a known command **[reported — via a peer-reviewed survey chapter; the 1989 primary is paywalled and I did not read it]**. (ii) _Enforced length_. VA.gov's design system states "keep button labels to 35 characters or fewer" with a 50-character absolute maximum enforced by the component **[verified — documented and implemented]**.

**Is there evidence that action labels and object labels produce different selection behaviour, error rates, or hesitation?** **No.** I found no controlled comparison of verb-labelled against noun-labelled options measuring selection, error rate, time, or abandonment, for humans. What exists is (a) _ordering_ guidance (noun-then-verb vs verb-then-noun interaction sequences — a different construct, about command grammar rather than labelling), argued from locus-of-attention reasoning and citing the 1987–1995 platform guidelines **[reported — Raskin, secondary access]**; (b) style rules; (c) scent theory.

**Does any of it distinguish "do the thing" from "give me the thing"?** **No.** The distinction does exist in the HCI record, but as an _interface-structure_ question — object-focused vs task-focused navigation — and the sources are opinion pieces on both sides: one argues objects are the primary mental model and tasks add a layer, another argues users arrive with tasks and that successful sites use nouns (`Calendar`, `Flights`) rather than verbs (`Schedule`, `Book`) **[speculative — both are design argument, no measurement]**. This is the closest the HCI literature comes to the lead, and it is an unresolved design disagreement, not a finding.

### 3.2 LLM tool and function selection

This is the decisive block, and it contains the most important negative result in the report.

**Tool names barely matter; position does.** The study that isolates metadata factors one at a time — tool name, server name, description, parameter schema, with positions balanced and conditional logistic regression for position-adjusted effects, five LLMs × five tasks, over metadata from 367,174 MCP tools — reports: **"Tool names rarely matter: only 6 of 29 numerical TT estimates have intervals that exclude zero."** Under tool-name-only comparisons, the target's selection rate "stays near the uniform-choice baseline", while **GPT, Claude, DeepSeek and Qwen select the first-listed tool in 78.7%–96.0% of trials** **[verified — measured, single study, preprint 2610.14334]**. The rank order of levers is: **parameter schema (largest) > description (next) > name (weakest, inconsistent)**.

**And where a name effect was found, description explained it away.** Renaming a _parameter_ to `recipient_id` dropped selection to 1–10%, while `receiver` held at 35–51%. But the paper's own mediation test is the transferable finding: "**the penalty for `recipient_id` thus stems from how models interpret the name: once both fields carry the same description, the name no longer matters**" **[verified — measured]**. Read carefully, this is the conditional-ambiguity refinement in miniature: a label moves behaviour only where the surrounding text does not already fix the reading.

**Description quality is the real lever, and its effect is conditional on brief specificity.** Two independent studies: 856 tools across 103 MCP servers, **97.1% carrying at least one "smell"**, 56% failing to state purpose, 89.3% missing usage guidance; augmenting all components raises median task success by **+5.85 pp** and partial goal completion by 15.12%, at **+67.46% execution steps** and **regression in 16.67% of cases** **[verified — measured, 2602.14878]**. Separately, 10,831 servers with 73% exhibiting repeated/non-distinct tool names; a controlled mutation study finds functionality (+11.6%, p<0.001) and accuracy (+8.8%, p<0.001) dominate, completeness matters most for complex queries, conciseness least (+1.5%); and **"functionality and accuracy [play] dominant roles under underspecified queries, and completeness [becomes] critical as query specificity increases"** — with standard-compliant descriptions reaching 72% selection against a 20% baseline in competitive settings **[verified — measured, 2602.18914]**.

That conditional sentence is the single most useful external result for the programme's refinement: **which property of a description matters depends on how specific the brief is.** It is not a label-form finding, and it does not rescue the lead, but it is the same _shape_ of hypothesis and it was tested and confirmed in an adjacent domain.

**Near-duplicate and competing tools are a measured, first-class problem.** ToolScope reports that semantically redundant tools with overlapping names and descriptions "introduce ambiguity and degrade tool selection performance", with merging-plus-filtering gains of 34.6% (Seal-Tools), 38.6% (UltraTool) and 8.8% (BFCL) **[verified — peer-reviewed, ACL 2026]**. ToolScan finds structurally similar API names are associated with lower accuracy, specifically on the _insufficient-call_ metric — models "misinterpret similar API names" **[verified — measured preprint 2411.13547]**. And candidate-set _filtering_ remains the largest single lever on record: 13.62% → **43.13%** selection accuracy with prompt tokens cut from 2133.84 to 1084.0 **[verified — measured, 2505.03275, small-scale]**.

**One direct contradiction inside this block.** An interface-drift ablation reports that "minor description rewording and semantics-preserving parameter renames do not meaningfully degrade accuracy" and that "the observed failure mode is candidate-set confusion" **[verified — measured, 2610.23342]**. This is consistent with "names rarely matter" and inconsistent with a large name effect; it contradicts the `recipient_id` result only if the renames are treated as semantically neutral, which the metadata study shows they are not. Logged as C1.

**Is abstention / refusal-to-choose documented as a failure mode distinct from wrong selection? Yes — and it is documented as a _mismeasured_ one.** Berkeley's benchmark expects it explicitly: the Missing-Parameters category tests "the model's ability to recognize when essential information is missing… In these cases, the model should request clarification rather than making unwarranted assumptions", and Missing-Functions requires recognising that no available function fits **[verified — benchmark documentation]**. The evaluation paper treats abstention as a first-class axis ("evaluates the ability of models to abstain") **[reported — abstract]**.

Then the decisive critique: **the official scorer never looks at the should-ask turn.** "The scorer skips that turn: its gold trajectory is empty, so there is nothing to compare, and whether the model asked or guessed is never checked… **asking earns nothing, guessing costs nothing on that turn, and asking twice loses the item**." Measured consequences: on 223 paired turns, GPT-5.4 attempts the call on 83.4% of complete turns and holds back on 78.0% of incomplete ones — **80.7% decision accuracy, best of seven models — while the official score ranks it sixth**. An added line telling it _not to ask_ produces "no detectable change" in decision accuracy but **+13.5 to 23.5 points of official score**; the opposite line, telling a Gemma model to ask first, improves decision accuracy by 4.5 points and **gains no official score**. "**The score moves with the push toward action, not with the decision**" **[verified — measured, 2610.04429]**. Per-category decision accuracy across seven models sits at 65.5–82.2% **[verified]**.

**What predicts abstention, as far as anything measures it.** Three predictors, in descending evidential strength: (i) **the metric** — if asking is not scored, asking decays; (ii) **explicit prompt instructions** — "use your judgment", "always call a tool first" move the act/ask boundary, documented by the vendor as steerable **[verified — documented]**; (iii) **model identity and the kind of thing missing** — models are consistently slightly worse at noticing a missing _function_ than a missing _parameter_ value **[verified — measured]**. **Label form is not among the documented predictors anywhere.**

Also worth recording, because it undermines the instrument class rather than the theory: an independent audit of 50 BFCL items found "almost half of the sampled questions had at least one defect", including **irrelevance items where the correct tool call is scored as wrong** — "What is the current time in Sydney, Australia?" with `get_local_time(location)` labelled irrelevant, so a model passes only by refusing **[verified — independent audit, Epoch AI]**. Abstention ground truth is noisy.

### 3.3 Nominalisation and task framing

**There is a linguistics result that bears directly on the pair, and it cuts against the brief's polarity.** Nominalisation is not a meaning-preserving category change: "-tion" nouns derived from verbs "can never be purely transpositional in the traditional sense: the very fact of changing category invariably presupposes some non-trivial semantic change", and the derived noun may denote the **event**, the **result state**, or another participant **[verified — documented in the morphology literature, Kawaletz & Plag 2015]**. Langacker's frame is the same point: nominalisation "shifts the profile" from process to thing **[reported]**.

Applied to the programme's actual stimuli: `explain` denotes the act. `explanation` denotes _either_ the act _or_ the artefact. **The noun form is the more semantically ambiguous one about answer type.** If a model is sensitive to that ambiguity at all, the noun entry should therefore attract _more_ clarification, not less — which is what the n=100 round found (noun+declarative asks most, 50/100) and what the brief's five draws suggested the reverse of.

**Nominalisation is also documented to change how a process is cognised** — "nominalizing a process should coerce its conceptualization toward a manipulable object category", making it appear more modular, stable and context-free **[speculative — the claim is a book-chapter proposal with supporting psych evidence, but I did not read the experiments, and nothing in it concerns interface labels]**. Action-identification theory supplies the compatible frame: the same action can be identified at a low (procedural) or high (outcome) level, and the level changes downstream behaviour **[reported — via secondary exposition]**. This is the best available _mechanism_ for the refinement: `explanation` is a higher-level identification than `explain`, so it may invite a different kind of completion. It remains a mechanism proposal, not a result.

**Does activity phrasing vs deliverable phrasing change behaviour? There is one documented case, and it is not about labels — it is about the brief.** Anthropic's prompt guidance states: "If you say 'can you suggest some changes,' Claude will sometimes provide suggestions rather than implementing them, even if making changes might be what you intended", with the paired remedy — "Less effective: _Can you suggest some changes to improve this function?_ / More effective: _Change this function to improve its performance._" **[verified — documented vendor behaviour, no measurement published]**. This is the exact artefact-versus-activity distinction the programme hypothesised, observed in production, by the vendor, with the polarity the refinement predicts: **naming the artefact gets you the artefact; naming the activity gets you the act.** It is the strongest external support the refinement has. It is also only an existence proof.

**Does ambiguity about the expected answer type produce clarifying questions?** Yes, and this is the best-instrumented literature in the report. CLAMBER's taxonomy contains a dedicated dimension, **Aleatoric Output** — "the input is well-formed but the output contains potential confusion due to the lack of essential elements", sub-categorised as missing _what / whom / when / where_ — which is precisely "ambiguous about what answer is wanted" **[verified — peer-reviewed, ACL 2024]**; its conclusions are that current models have "limited practical utility in identifying and clarifying ambiguous user queries, even enhanced by chain-of-thought and few-shot prompting", and that those techniques "may result in overconfidence" **[verified — measured, ~12K items]**.

And the counterweight: **models recognise ambiguity far more often than they act on it.** In a three-setting design (QA, explicit ambiguity judgement, behavioural classification into direct answer / refusal / clarifying question), "models often identify ambiguity when explicitly asked to judge it, yet in the QA setting they overwhelmingly default to direct answers", and **retrieved context widens the gap** by improving answerability while making clarifying questions even less likely **[verified — measured, 2605.25284]**. Additionally, LLM prompt-sensitivity work establishes the base rate for "surface wording matters": up to **76 accuracy points** of swing across meaning-preserving _formatting_ variants for LLaMA-2-13B, **median spread 7.5 points**, with format performance only weakly correlated between models **[verified — measured, ICLR 2024, 2310.11324]**. Any small label-form effect must be read against a variance floor of this order.

## 4. The four questions answered

**Q1. Is there prior art for "a verb-named entry routes differently from a noun-named entry" in a list of options?**
**No — not for the claim as stated.** No study compares verb-named with noun-named entries while holding everything else fixed, in any domain I could reach. The nearest measurements are (a) tool-name interventions, where **names rarely matter (6/29 intervals exclude zero) and position dominates when names are the only difference** — this _contradicts_ a general form effect and is compatible only with a conditional one; (b) name effects that **vanish when descriptions are equalised** — which supports the refinement's mechanism without supporting the label-form claim; (c) HCI scent findings, which predict _specificity_ effects and are silent on grammatical form. **Population and effect size: five LLMs, five tasks, 78.7–96.0% first-listed selection under name-only differences. Human population: none measured.** Verdict on the refinement: **left open by the literature, and the only factor-isolated study available leans against it.**

**Q2. Is abstention / clarification-seeking documented as a selection failure mode, distinct from mis-selection? What predicts it?**
**Yes, unambiguously, and as a decision rather than a failure.** BFCL builds two of four multi-turn categories around turns where the model should ask, and defines asking as the expected response **[verified — documentation]**. It is distinct from mis-selection in the strongest possible sense: it is a separate turn type with its own gold label, and the standard scorer **does not score it at all**, so asking earns nothing and guessing is free **[verified — measured]**. Predictors, in order of evidence: the scoring rule (dominant); explicit prompt instruction (documented as steerable, and shown to move score without moving decision quality); and model capability, 65.5–82.2% decision accuracy. In the QA setting, the predictor is **whether the task affords abstention at all** — models default to answering **[verified]**. **Label form does not appear as a predictor anywhere in this literature.**

**Q3. Does any literature tie ambiguity about answer type to clarifying questions?**
**Yes, twice, from opposite directions.** CLAMBER names it as a taxonomy dimension (Aleatoric Output: well-formed input, under-specified expected output) and shows models handle it poorly **[verified]**. "Knowing but Not Showing" shows the recognition-behaviour gap, and that better retrieval _reduces_ asking **[verified]**. And the one documented activity-vs-artefact case in production — "suggest some changes" yielding suggestions rather than edits — is a request-level instance of the same mechanism **[verified — documented]**. **Nothing ties _label_ ambiguity about answer type to clarifying questions**, which is the specific link the programme's refinement asserts.

**Q4. Are there published naming rules backed by measurement rather than style opinion?**
**Almost none, and the exceptions are not about grammar.** Measurement-backed or enforcement-backed rules I could verify: _filter the candidate set_ (13.62% → 43.13%, tokens halved); _merge redundant tools_ (+8.8 to +38.6%); _make the description state function and accuracy before anything else_ (+11.6%, +8.8%); _make completeness scale with brief specificity_; _cap length where a component enforces it_ (VA.gov 35/50 chars); _namespace by service_ (vendor claims "non-trivial effects", **no numbers published**); _standardise the name format_ (MCP SEP-986: 1–64 chars, case-sensitive, charset, uniqueness — a **format** rule with no semantic content) **[verified]**. Unbacked rules, universally stated and never measured: verb-first names; "one action per tool"; "avoid generic verbs"; "describe what and when" (Agent Skills requires it in the spec, and its own optimisation page prescribes _measuring trigger accuracy_ rather than asserting a rule) **[verified — the requirement; reported — that it improves outcomes]**.

## 7. What this means for our lead

**Verdict: unattested — and the nearest evidence is unsupportive of a general form effect, supportive only of a conditional one.**

Three separate readings converge:

1. **The external literature has no form-effect result to corroborate.** The factor-isolated study says names are the weakest of four levers and that position dominates name-only comparisons. Scent theory predicts specificity effects and is indifferent to part of speech. The verb-first convention is universal and unmeasured. This is the classic shape of a belief that survives because it has never been tested, not because it passed.
2. **The mechanism the refinement needs exists, and it points at descriptions rather than labels.** The name effect that _was_ found disappeared when descriptions were equalised. Description-conditionality was then independently confirmed as a _specificity_ interaction. If the programme wants a rule, the literature's rule is: **the line does the work, and the label is a fallback.**
3. **The programme's own corrected data already reversed the brief's direction**, and the morphology literature predicts that reversal: `explanation` is ambiguous between event and result, `explain` is not. A noun label is not a _more_ specific label; for this pair it is a less specific one.

**What to say, and what not to say.** Do not say "nouns beat verbs" (the brief already forbids it; the n=100 data now contradicts it). Do not say "label form is second-order" as though that were the finding — it is a null on a design the log itself records as structurally uninformative, because the control pair sat at 100/100 ([`modes-experiment-log.md`](modes-experiment-log.md:379), Guard 2 fired). The honest summary is: **label form is unattested externally and untested internally at a design that could have detected it.**

**The next measurement.** One experiment, and it is not the one the programme has been running.

> **Hold the brief fixed at maximal ambiguity and vary only whether the entry line states the artefact.** Three arms, n = 100 each, same slug, same name, same position, randomised order across draws: (A) activity-only line — "Use when someone asks why code behaves as it does"; (B) artefact-stating line — "…; returns a written explanation of the cause"; (C) activity line plus explicit non-artefact disambiguator — "…; does not produce a written explanation". Primary outcome: **ask-rate**, not hit-rate, because the only large measured effect in this programme is an asking effect (`.427` vs `.995`, p < 10⁻⁴⁰) and the external literature says asking is where the mismeasurement lives. Secondary: hit-rate. Pre-register that A and B are expected to differ on asking and not on hitting — that is the refinement's falsifiable content, and it does not depend on a verb/noun contrast at all.

Reason to prefer this over another label-form round: it tests the refinement's _mechanism_ (does the label's answer-type information change asking?) rather than its _symptom_ (does the part of speech change the pick?), it is powered against the effect the programme has actually observed, and a null is interpretable because the control is not at ceiling.

## 8. Suggested naming rules, with evidence and falsifier

Each rule below is offered at the strength the evidence supports. None is a finding.

**R1 — Put the answer type in the line, not in the name.** _Evidence:_ name effect vanishes when descriptions are equalised **[verified]**; description function/accuracy dominates selection **[verified, +11.6%/+8.8%]**; documented artefact-vs-activity behaviour at request level **[verified — vendor]**. _Falsifier:_ at n=100, an artefact-stating line produces no reduction in ask-rate against an activity-only line on an ambiguous brief.

**R2 — Prefer the activity verb when the entry performs, and the noun when the entry hands back an artefact the caller then uses — and only then.** _Evidence:_ **none directly.** This is the refinement, restated as a rule, and the literature neither supports nor refutes it (§3.1, §3.2). _Falsifier:_ the §7 experiment, or any label-form round with a non-ceiling control.

**R3 — One entry, one answer type. Do not let one entry be readable as both act and artefact.** _Evidence:_ morphological ambiguity of `-tion` nouns between event and result readings **[verified — linguistics]**; aleatoric-output ambiguity is a recognised, poorly-handled class **[verified]**; near-duplicate/overlapping entries measurably degrade selection **[verified]**. _Falsifier:_ an entry that is deliberately readable both ways shows no elevated ask-rate relative to one that is not.

**R4 — Spend the budget on discriminability, not on grammatical form.** _Evidence:_ candidate-set filtering is the largest recorded lever (13.62% → 43.13%) **[verified]**; tool merging gains 8.8–38.6% **[verified, peer-reviewed]**; forced parallel language is a documented mistake and vague verbs specifically fail **[reported]**; the strongest single study finds names the weakest lever **[verified]**. _Falsifier:_ a catalogue whose only change is part-of-speech harmonisation outperforms one whose only change is de-duplication, at equal cost.

**R5 — Cap the line length; treat the cap as policy, not measurement.** _Evidence:_ every published cap is enforced and none is derived from a compliance study — MCP names 1–64 chars, Agent Skills description 1,024, VA.gov button 35/50 **[verified — documented]**; no source measures an optimum for this surface **[no data]**. _Falsifier:_ a study showing compliance degrades monotonically past a specific length would convert policy into evidence. Until then, do not present the number as a finding — this is already the position in [`modes-architecture.md`](../modes-architecture.md:238).

**R6 — If abstention matters, make it a scored outcome with a legal output.** _Evidence:_ asking decays when unscored, and the score tracks the push toward action rather than the decision **[verified — measured]**; the programme's own abstention column read 0/100 as behaviour when it was an artefact **[verified — internal]**. _Falsifier:_ a scaffold that scores asking and still shows asking collapse.

**R7 — Do not transfer human menu findings to language models without saying so.** _Evidence:_ none of the HCI results cited here were obtained from a model; none of the model results were obtained from people; the affordances differ (a menu is clicked, a tool is called, a mode is switched into) **[speculative — methodological]**. _Falsifier:_ a study running both populations on the same stimuli.

## 10. What could not be checked

- **No replication exists for any quantitative claim in §3.** The three preprints carrying the most weight (S1, S2, S5) are single studies. S1's "names rarely matter" is the load-bearing negative result in this report and it rests on one design, one family of tasks, and five models. If it is wrong, R4 weakens and the lead is merely untested rather than tending against.
- **The relevant HCI experiment may exist under vocabulary I did not search.** Fields name this construct "command naming", "menu item labelling", "action/object wording", "task- vs object-oriented navigation". I searched those. I did not search the gerund/nominal-form literature in second-language acquisition or the survey-methodology literature on response-option phrasing, both of which plausibly contain verb/noun comparisons. Absence of evidence here is bounded by that.
- **No human data of any kind.** Every claim about hesitation, abandonment or clarification-seeking in a chooser is either from a model or from a non-chooser task. §8's R7 is the only honest treatment available.
- **The programme's own abstention classifier is described in its log as heuristic and provisional** ([`modes-experiment-log.md`](modes-experiment-log.md:449), "**provisional** — heuristic classifier"). The corrected ask-rates in §0 are therefore better than the artefact they replaced but are not validated instruments. Every number quoted from the programme in this report inherits that caveat.
- **Vendor internal measurements are unauditable.** Anthropic's "non-trivial effects" for namespacing and its SWE-bench description-refinement result are stated without designs, sample sizes, or effect sizes, and cannot be reproduced from published material.
- **Two figures in this repository's own documentation rest on single studies that I could not strengthen.** [`modes-architecture.md`](../modes-architecture.md:36) and [`:206`](../modes-architecture.md:206) cite the RAG-MCP and description-enrichment numbers as though settled; both are single-study. They are correctly labelled elsewhere in that document, but the summary framing reads more confidently than the evidence supports. Recording it here rather than editing it, per the brief's instruction that nothing else be modified.
